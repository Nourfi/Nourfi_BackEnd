**What makes an Islamic financing different from a regular bank loan**

- **How the money is earned**
  A conventional loan charges *interest* (riba) on the amount borrowed. The bank’s profit comes from the interest rate applied to the loan balance.
  In many Islamic products the bank does **not** charge interest. Instead it earns a **profit markup** on a sale of a real asset (Murabahah) or a rental fee for using an asset (Ijarah). The profit is agreed in advance and is not linked to the passage of time alone [Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].

- **Underlying transaction**
  Islamic financing is structured as a **sale or lease** of a tangible good or service, not as a pure loan of money. For example, in a Murabahah the bank buys a commodity and then sells it to the customer at a higher price, with the customer paying later. The profit margin is fixed at the start [Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].
  In an Ijarah the bank buys an asset (e.g., a car or equipment) and leases it to the customer; the customer pays rent and may later acquire ownership. The lease payments are not considered interest [Source : AAOIFI Standard 9].

- **Risk sharing**
  Some Islamic contracts, such as Mudarabah, pair a capital provider with an entrepreneur who manages a business. The profit is shared according to a pre‑agreed ratio, and loss is borne by the capital provider unless caused by negligence [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13]. This contrasts with a conventional loan where the borrower bears all business risk while the lender receives interest regardless of outcome.

- **Prohibited guarantees**
  Islamic standards forbid guaranteeing the capital amount (a promise to buy partnership assets at face value) because it would act like an interest guarantee [Source : AAOIFI Standard 12]. Regular loans often include such guarantees (e.g., collateral that ensures repayment of the exact principal plus interest).

- **Legal basis**
  Islamic finance follows AAOIFI standards that define each contract (Murabahah, Ijarah, Mudarabah, etc.) and set conditions to keep them Shari’ah‑compliant. Conventional banking follows civil law and central‑bank regulations, which allow interest‑based lending.

In short, an Islamic financing replaces the interest‑based loan with a sale, lease, or profit‑sharing arrangement that involves a real asset and a pre‑agreed profit, while a regular bank loan simply lends money for a fee calculated as interest.

**Sources**: Murabahah definition and profit markup [Source : AAOIFI Standard 8]; Ijarah lease structure and prohibition of interest‑like rent increases [Source : AAOIFI Standard 9]; Mudarabah profit‑sharing principle and contrast with interest‑based banking [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13]; prohibition of capital guarantees in partnership contracts [Source : AAOIFI Standard 12].

**Definition of Ijārah Muntahiyah bi‑t‑Tamleek**
Ijārah Muntahiyah bi‑t‑Tamleek is a specific form of leasing used by Islamic financial institutions. It is a leasing contract that **includes a promise by the lessor to transfer ownership of the leased asset to the lessee** at the end of the lease period. This distinguishes it from a plain ijārah, which only provides a usufruct (right to use) without any transfer of title [Source : AAOIFI Standard 9].
