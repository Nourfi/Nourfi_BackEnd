**How a Murabaha financing works (simple terms)**
A Murabaha is a sale‑by‑the‑bank. The bank first buys the item the client wants (a car, a house, equipment, etc.). Then the bank sells that item to the client at the purchase price **plus a disclosed profit margin**. The client pays the bank in installments or at once. The profit amount must be stated up front, just like a markup on a conventional loan. The bank’s role is a sale, not a loan, and the client receives ownership of the asset when the sale is completed [Source : AAOIFI Standard 37].

**Risks for the client (the buyer)**
- The client must pay the agreed price plus profit even if the market price of the asset falls later.
- If the client cannot meet the payment schedule, the bank may enforce the contract or take back the asset, because the bank owns the asset until full payment is received.

**Risks for the bank (the financier)**
- The bank’s profit depends on the client’s ability to pay; default means the bank may lose the profit portion.
- To protect its capital, the bank often deals only with parties that have strong credit ratings, so that the combination of the principal amount and the Murabaha profit helps preserve the initial capital [Source : AAOIFI Standard 45].
- The bank may set a **credit facility limit** that caps the total amount it can sell under Murabaha to a single client, reducing exposure to any one transaction [Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].

In summary, Murabaha works like a cost‑plus sale with a fixed profit. The buyer risks paying a set price regardless of market changes, while the bank risks non‑payment and therefore uses credit limits and selects credit‑worthy clients to manage that risk [Source : AAOIFI Standard 37][Source : AAOIFI Standard 45][Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].

Murabaha is a sale‑based financing where the Islamic financial institution purchases an asset on behalf of the client and then resells it to the client at a disclosed cost‑plus profit margin. The cost that the institution incurred for obtaining the good must be indicated, distinguishing it from Musawamah where the cost is not disclosed [Source : AAOIFI Standard 37]. The transaction is treated as a credit facility: the client receives an upper limit for Murabaha transactions, which may be restricted to a specific type of item or a defined period [Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].

**Risks for the institution (financier)**
1. **Credit risk** – the client may default on the deferred payment agreed in the Murabaha contract. To mitigate this, institutions often allocate the portion of capital used for Murabaha to counterparties that have strong credit ratings, so that the combination of principal and profit protects the initial capital [Source : AAOIFI Standard 45].
2. **Operational/agency risk** – if the institution acts through an agent (e.g., a wakalah arrangement) and the agent purchases the asset at a price higher than the market price or the price set by the principal, the institution must compensate the principal for the excess amount [Source : AAOIFI Standard 23].

**Risks for the client (buyer)**
1. **Payment risk** – the client is obligated to pay the agreed price plus profit on the deferred schedule; failure to do so may lead to breach of contract.
2. **Asset‑price risk** – because the cost price is disclosed, the client bears the risk that the market value of the asset may fall below the purchase price plus profit, leaving the client with an over‑priced asset.

In syndicated Murabaha arrangements, multiple financial institutions may participate, with one party assuming leadership of the deal; each participant retains an independent liability separate from its own company, which limits the spread of risk among the syndicate members [Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].

These points summarize the mechanism and the principal risk exposures for both parties as reflected in the AAOIFI excerpts. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 8][Source : AAOIFI Standard 45][Source : AAOIFI Standard 37][Source : AAOIFI Standard 23]

**Risks for the client (buyer)**
* Payment risk – the client must meet the agreed‑upon resale price (cost + profit) on the schedule stipulated in the contract; failure to do so results in default and possible loss of the asset.
* Market risk – because the resale price is fixed at the time of the contract, the client bears any subsequent decline in the market value of the asset; the profit margin does not adjust to market conditions.

Thus, Murabaha transfers ownership of the asset to the client immediately, while the financing risk is primarily credit risk for the institution and payment/market risk for the client [Source : AAOIFI Standard 37][Source : AAOIFI Standard 45][Source : AAOIFI Standard 23].
