**Q:** What are the conditions for a valid murabaha sale?

retrieval: mode=normal best_sim=0.860 standards=8 8 8 8 8  


**A:**

A valid murabaha sale must meet several clear conditions:

1. **The institution must own the asset before it can sell it.**
   The bank (or its agent) has to purchase the good from the supplier and acquire title or constructive possession first; it cannot conclude a murabaha contract with the customer before it has done so[Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].

2. **The purchase price and the profit margin must be known and fixed at the time the sale contract is signed.**
   Both the cost price and the institution’s markup have to be disclosed and cannot be left to future changes or linked to uncertain rates such as LIBOR. The total selling price may be mentioned, but the exact profit amount must also be specified[Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].

3. **Transparency about the supplier’s offer is required.**
   The customer may obtain a price statement from the supplier. If the statement is addressed to the institution (an offer to the institution), the institution can accept it and the sale is deemed concluded when the institution gives its acceptance[Source : AAOIFI Standard 8]. A general price statement without a named customer is only an invitation to negotiate, not an offer.

4. **The contract must be documented in a signed agreement or standard application form.**
   The customer signs a document prepared by the institution that records the agreed price, profit, and other terms[Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].

5. **Defect and recourse clauses are addressed.**
   The institution is responsible for hidden defects that appear after the contract unless it expressly states otherwise, and it may give the customer the right to claim compensation from the supplier for such defects[Source : AAOIFI Standard 8].

These elements together ensure the murabaha sale complies with the AAOIFI standards.

**A:**

**Quelles sont les conditions de la mudâraba ?**

- **Deux parties capables juridiquement** : il faut un investisseur (donneur de capital) et un entrepreneur (mudârib) qui ont tous deux la capacité légale d’agir comme agents et d’accepter un mandat [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13].

- **Existence d’un accord écrit** : si le contrat est conclu sur la base d’un protocole d’accord, le contenu de ce protocole devient partie intégrante du contrat, sauf si les parties ont expressément exclu certaines obligations [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13].

- **Liberté ou restriction de l’activité** :
  * *Mudâraba non restreinte* : l’investisseur laisse le mudârib gérer les fonds sans restriction, en se fiant à son expertise, mais toujours dans le respect des intérêts des deux parties et de l’objectif de profit [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13].
  * *Mudâraba restreinte* : l’investisseur peut limiter les secteurs (services, commerce, une marchandise ou un groupe de marchandises) à condition que les marchandises soient couramment disponibles, non rares, non saisonnières et en offre suffisante pour atteindre les objectifs du contrat [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13].

- **Intervention du donneur de capital** : le donneur de capital ne peut pas imposer son droit d’intervenir dans les activités d’achat, de vente ou de fourniture du mudârib [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13].

- **Durée du contrat** : le contrat peut prévoir une limite de temps, car il est considéré comme un contrat d’agence qui peut être fixé pour une durée déterminée [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13].

- **Caractère non contraignant avant le démarrage** : avant le début des travaux, le contrat est considéré comme non contraignant et chaque partie peut le résilier unilatéralement [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13].

Ces points constituent les principales conditions que la norme AAOIFI (Standard 13) impose pour qu’une mudâraba soit valide.

**Q:** How does salam differ from istisna?

retrieval: mode=normal best_sim=0.853 standards=10 11 10 10 10  


**A:**

**What is Salam?**
Salam is a sale where the buyer pays the full price right away and the seller promises to deliver a specific, tangible commodity at a later date. The contract can be called “Salam”, “Salaf”, “sale” or any term that shows a sale of a prescribed commodity for deferred delivery in exchange for immediate payment [Source : AAOIFI Standard 10].

**What is Istisna’a?**
Istisna’a is a contract for manufacturing or construction. The seller (manufacturer or builder) must provide both the service of making the item and the materials needed, and then deliver the finished product to the buyer [Source : AAOIFI Standard 11].

**Key differences**

| Aspect | Salam | Istisna’a |
|--------|-------|-----------|
| **What is sold** | A pre‑specified, existing commodity (e.g., wheat, metal) that will be delivered later. | A product that does not yet exist; it is manufactured or built after the contract is signed. |
| **Payment timing** | Buyer pays the whole price immediately; delivery is deferred. | Payment terms can be flexible (often staged) and are linked to the manufacturing/construction progress. |
| **Seller’s obligations** | Deliver the exact commodity at the agreed future date; no obligation to produce it. | Produce the item and supply the necessary materials; the seller creates the product. |
| **Contract classification** | Covered by AAOIFI Standard 10 (Salam and Parallel Salam). | Covered by AAOIFI Standard 11 (Istisna’a and Parallel Istisna’a); it is not covered by the Salam standard [Source : AAOIFI Standard 10][Source : AAOIFI Standard 11]. |

In short, Salam is a forward sale of an existing good with payment up front, while Istisna’a is a contract to make or build something that does not yet exist, with the seller handling both production and material supply [Source : AAOIFI Standard 11][Source : AAOIFI Standard 10].

**Comment calculer votre Zakat sur l’or et l’argent ?**

1. **Déterminez ce que vous possédez**
   - Faites le total du poids net d’or et d’argent que vous détenez, ou bien la valeur monétaire actuelle de ces métaux (prix du marché).
   - Le calcul peut se faire soit en kilogrammes, soit en argent / dollar, tant que vous utilisez la même base pour tout le calcul. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 35]

2. **Vérifiez le seuil de nisab**
   - Le nisab (seuil minimum) pour les métaux précieux correspond à la valeur de **85 kg d’or**.

3. **Appliquez le taux de Zakat**
   - Si votre possession dépasse le nisab, la Zakat s’élève à **2,5 %** du poids net ou de la valeur totale. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 35]

4. **Exemple simplifié (sans chiffres précis)**
   - Vous avez 90 kg d’or → vous êtes au-dessus du nisab, donc vous payez 2,5 % de 90 kg.
   - Vous avez 200 000 € d’argent → vérifiez que cette valeur dépasse la valeur de 85 kg d’or ; si oui, payez 2,5 % de 200 000 €.

En résumé : **(poids ou valeur) – > vérifier le nisab (85 kg d’or) – > si dépassé, payer 2,5 %**. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 35]

**Q:** What is a sukuk and what kinds of assets can it represent?

retrieval: mode=normal best_sim=0.874 standards=17 17 39 17 17  


**A:**

A sukuk is a certificate that gives its holder a common share in the ownership of an underlying asset, rather than a loan‑based claim. The holder owns part of the asset (or the right to its income) and shares any profit or loss in proportion to the certificates they hold. It is not a debt owed by the issuer. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 17]

Investment sukuk can be linked to many types of Shari’ah‑permissible assets, including:

* **Non‑monetary assets** such as tangible property or equipment.
* **Usufructs** (the right to use and benefit from an asset).
* **Services** provided by an asset.
* **Mixtures** of the above together with intangible rights, debts and monetary assets.

The standard also lists specific categories of investment sukuk that represent ownership of:

* Leased assets,
* Usufructs,
* Services,
* Murabahah, Salam, Istisna’a, Mudarabah, Musharakah, investment agency, share‑cropping (Muzara’ah), irrigation (Musaqat) and agricultural partnerships.

These sukuk do not represent shares of joint‑stock companies, fund certificates, or investment portfolios. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 17]

**A:**

Here are the main business‑finance methods that AAOIFI has standards for, explained in plain language:

**1. Murabahah** – A “cost‑plus” sale. The institution buys an asset, then sells it to the customer at the purchase price plus an agreed profit margin. The transaction moves ownership from the seller to the buyer, and the profit is paid in installments if agreed. This is the Shari’ah‑compliant alternative to a conventional loan for buying goods. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 8]

**2. Ijarah (and Ijarah Muntahia Bittamleek)** – An Islamic lease. The institution owns an asset and rents it to the customer. If the lease ends with the customer buying the asset, the rent is only for the portion the customer does not already own. The lease can be arranged through an agent who acquires the asset on the institution’s behalf. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 9]

**3. Istisna’a (and Parallel Istisna’a)** – A “make‑to‑order” contract. The institution agrees to manufacture or construct an asset and deliver it later. Payment can be made in stages or deferred, and the price may be paid in instalments as the work progresses. It is often used for building projects or equipment manufacturing. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 11]

**4. Salam (and Parallel Salam)** – A forward sale of a specific, measurable commodity for delivery at a future date. The buyer pays the full price now, giving the seller working capital (for example, a farmer needing cash to plant crops). The contract specifies the exact quantity, quality and delivery date. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 10]

**5. Mudarabah** – A profit‑sharing partnership where one party provides capital and the other provides expertise and manages the business. Profits are shared according to a pre‑agreed ratio; losses are borne by the capital provider unless caused by negligence. Valuation is based on actual or fair value, without applying interest. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 13]

**6. Musharakah (Sharikah)** – An equity partnership where all partners contribute cash or tangible assets. Profits are shared as agreed, which may differ from each partner’s capital share; losses are shared in proportion to each partner’s equity stake. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 12]

**7. Investment Sukuk** – Certificates that represent ownership of a leased asset (or the usufruct of an existing asset). Holders become owners of the asset and receive rental income, similar to bond‑like securities but backed by real assets. [Source : AAOIFI Standard 17]

These are the AAOIFI‑defined structures used for business financing. If you need details on any other method not listed here, the excerpts do not cover it.

**Q:** Explain musharakah and how profit and loss are shared between partners

retrieval: mode=normal best_sim=0.859 standards=12 12 12 12 12  


**A:**

Musharakah is a partnership where two or more people put money or assets together to run a business.
Each partner’s share of the capital can be cash, land, equipment, or anything that can be given a monetary value[Source : AAOIFI Standard 12].

**How profit is shared**
The profit (or income) each partner receives must be agreed on in the contract.
Normally the profit is divided according to the percentage of each partner’s capital, but the partners may also agree on a profit‑sharing ratio that is **different** from the capital ratio, as long as a “sleeping” partner (one who does not work) does not get a larger share than allowed[Source : AAOIFI Standard 12].

**How loss is shared**
Losses must be borne **in proportion to each partner’s capital contribution**. The loss‑sharing ratio cannot be changed to make one partner or a group of partners bear all the loss[Source : AAOIFI Standard 12].

In short, you put in your share of the money or assets, you get a share of the profit that can be set by agreement, and you bear any loss in line with how much you contributed.

[Source : AAOIFI Standard 12]

**Q:** I want to buy a car with murabaha. Isn't it just a normal bank loan with another name?

retrieval: mode=normal best_sim=0.837 standards=8 37 16 37 8  


**A:**

A Murabaha is not a normal bank loan.
In a Murabaha the institution **buys the car first**, then sells it to you at a price that includes the purchase cost **plus a disclosed profit margin**. The cost you pay for the car must be shown clearly, and the profit is agreed up front. This is a sale transaction, not a loan of money that you later repay with interest.

Because the transaction is a sale, the Shari’ah rules forbid treating it as a “sale of debt” or as a simple promise to pay later; the profit margin must be fixed and cannot become a rescheduled debt. These features differentiate Murabaha from a conventional loan where only money is lent and interest is charged on the amount owed.

So, while both provide you with the means to own a car, a Murabaha works through a purchase‑and‑resale structure with a known profit, whereas a conventional loan is a pure credit arrangement with interest.

[Source : AAOIFI Standard 37][Source : AAOIFI Standard 8]

**Q:** Murabaha or ijarah: which structure fits buying a house?

retrieval: mode=normal best_sim=0.857 standards=8 9 46 37 45  


**A:**

Both structures can be used for a house, but they work differently.

**Murabaha** – This is a sale where the institution buys the house, takes ownership and the risk, then sells it to you at a disclosed cost plus a profit margin. The standard notes that Murabaha is used by Islamic institutions for “owning moveable as well as immovable assets” such as a house [Source : AAOIFI Standard 37].

**Ijarah** – This is a lease. The institution retains ownership of the house and you pay rent for the right to use it. The definition says Ijarah is “leasing of property … a specified permissible benefit … for a specified period in return for a specified permissible consideration” [Source : AAOIFI Standard 9]. A special form, Ijarah Muntahia Bittamleek, can include a promise to transfer title later, but the basic Ijarah keeps ownership with the lessor until a separate sale is arranged.

**Which fits?**
- If you want to become the outright owner from the start, a Murabaha structure matches that goal.
- If you prefer to lease first and possibly acquire ownership later, an Ijarah (or Ijarah Muntahia Bittamleek) is the appropriate structure.

**Q:** Can the mudarib guarantee the capital of the investor in mudaraba?

retrieval: mode=normal best_sim=0.877 standards=13 45 13 13 13  


**A:**

The mudarib cannot give an unconditional guarantee that the investor’s capital will be returned.

In a mudarabah the mudarib is **liable for the amount of the capital** only in the sense that he must manage the funds responsibly, but he is **not liable for any loss of capital** unless the loss is caused by his **wilful misconduct, negligence or breach of the contract**. The standard also says it is not permissible to require the mudarib to be unconditionally liable for any loss of capital beyond those cases.

So, a guarantee that the investor will always get the original capital back cannot be included in a mudarabah contract.

**Q:** Quelle est la différence entre un sukuk et une action ?

retrieval: mode=normal best_sim=0.821 standards=17 17 17 17 17  


**A:**

**Différence simple :**

- **Sukuk** : ce sont des certificats qui donnent à leur détenteur une part de propriété sur un actif réel (un bien loué, un usufruit, un service, etc.). **En résumé** : un sukuk est un titre de propriété sur un actif conforme à la charia, tandis qu’une action est une part de capital d’une société qui ne relève pas du cadre des sukuk selon les extraits AAOIFI.
